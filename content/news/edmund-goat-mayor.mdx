There's a new personality working the crowd along Highway 64/74A this summer, and he doesn't own a suit, a podium, or — as far as anyone can tell — a single filing fee. His name is Edmund. He's a horned, shaggy-coated Kiko goat, he crosses the Rocky Broad River whenever the mood strikes him, and depending on which corner of the internet you ask, he is either Chimney Rock's most beloved stray or the strongest write-in candidate Lake Lure has seen in years.

Nobody planned any of this. It just sort of happened, the way the best small-town stories do.

## Where Edmund Came From

Edmund showed up a few weeks ago, unannounced, inside a goat fence belonging to Robin Gaskey Phillips, who lives along the highway across the river from Chimney Rock. She didn't know him, didn't own him, and didn't have a fence tall enough to keep him anywhere he didn't want to be.

"Apparently his other goat friends were captured by Henderson County," Phillips wrote in a Facebook post, "and since then he has evaded authorities. Maybe he knows to stay away from the county line?" She has no idea where he came from before that or what his life was like — only that he'd found her yard, her river, and her mountain, and decided that was close enough to home.

She named him after a New Zealand mountaineer and explorer, because it's a Kiko — a hardy meat-and-browse breed developed in New Zealand — and because, as she put it, "I take animal names pretty seriously, just ask my dogs Janis & Dolly." Locals also call him "Big Boy," for reasons that require no further explanation once you've seen his horns.

## A Goat With a Routine

Edmund's days follow a pattern by now. He crosses the river, climbs the stone steps near the road, works his way through a stand of kudzu, and — on quieter days, when construction noise doesn't send him elsewhere — makes his way back to Phillips' place to hang out. He is, by all accounts, doing exactly what he wants, whenever he wants, and the whole community seems to have made peace with that.

It hasn't all been smooth. In one close call, Edmund nearly wandered into a campground where Phillips' own female goats were being kept temporarily separate from him — he's still intact, and a vet visit to fix that (and safely fold him into the herd) is in the works. "Good thing Robin got there in time to prevent an unwanted pregnancy," neighbor Laura Deaton wrote, only half joking. "Edmund it's time for you to get home."

The bigger, more serious concern is the highway itself. Edmund likes to walk the shoulder near the one-lane bridge, and Phillips has been asking drivers — politely, repeatedly — to actually observe the posted 25 mph limit and watch for him the way they already watch for bears. "We just want to make sure he stays safe and everyone driving by stays safe," she wrote. One commenter suggested the road might need an actual goat crossing sign at this point, and honestly, it's hard to argue with her.

## Chimney Rock's Unofficial Mascot

Somewhere between the second and third viral post, Edmund stopped being "that goat people keep seeing" and became something closer to a local celebrity. Local videographer and muralist Andrea Martin posted a reel of Edmund set to Phil Collins' "In the Air Tonight" — timed, fittingly, to a bit of tree demolition — under the caption "Chimney Rock's Most Wanted Man," with the hashtags #FreeEdmund, #ChimneyRock, and #DontFenceMeIn. In it, she ran through "Things I've Learned from Edmund," including: don't let anybody fence you in, eat your greens (even if they're kudzu), be a little stubborn, and "be free, be happy, and let people wonder how you keep getting away with it."

The comments turned into their own small phenomenon. Someone suggested Edmund make an appearance as a therapy goat at a local hospital. Someone else, only sort of kidding, asked whether he keeps the bears away (Phillips says they haven't had a bear at the house since he arrived). And more than one person pointed out, correctly, that a goat who naturally slows highway traffic, eats invasive kudzu for free, and has never once raised his voice at a town meeting is already outperforming most of the field.

## From Meme to "Mayor"

That's the joke that stuck. In the Lake Lure Local Facebook group, resident Jonathan Hinkle debuted a full campaign-style graphic — a vintage badge portrait of Edmund over a mountain backdrop, "EDMUND FOR MAYOR" in bold western type, and a full mock campaign poster billing him as "Lake Lure's G.O.A.T. (Greatest Of All Time)," complete with a platform: "Head of the Graze Committee," "Chief of Trail Maintenance," "Expert in baa-geting," and the closing line, "I don't bleat empty promises."

The group loved it. "Best candidate yet," one commenter wrote. "A true GOAT! Will be better than we have now," wrote another. Someone floated Edmund for Vice Mayor instead, out of respect for the sitting officeholder. Several people, independently, asked the same question: where do we get the t-shirt?

This is a town having fun with itself, the way towns with a genuine sense of humor about their own politics tend to do. That's exactly why it's worth writing down: it's the most good-natured thing to happen in Lake Lure's political conversation in a while.

## What Happens to Edmund Now

The practical story is still unfolding. Phillips is working on getting Edmund vet-checked, neutered if it's safe to do so, and folded permanently into her herd — at which point he'd have an actual home rather than a river he crosses on his own schedule. Until then, the ask from everyone who knows him is simple: slow down near the bridge, give him room the way you would a bear or a deer, and don't try to feed him or load him into your car, no matter how good the photo opportunity looks.

In the meantime, Edmund has already done more for local morale than most single news stories manage. He's brought neighbors together in comment sections, turned a stray-animal situation into a running community joke, and reminded a town in the middle of some genuinely contentious politics that it's still capable of laughing at itself. That's worth something on its own — before you even get to the t-shirts.
