*Helene cut Chimney Rock Village off from Lake Lure's sewer. Its temporary replacement plant, upstream of the lake, has failed its pollution limits every month since July. Although the village says it doesn't want to reconnect to Lake Lure, it is negotiating to do just that.*

*This is the final part of a four-part series built from state permit files, EPA data and Town and Village records. [Part 1](/news/lake-lure-sewer-over-capacity) covered Lake Lure's plant running over its permitted flow; [Part 2](/news/lake-lure-sewer-mostly-lake-water) how the system got here; [Part 3](/news/lake-lure-sewer-2031-who-pays) the 2031 schedule and the $125 million bill. CHIMNEY ROCK, N.C. — For decades, Chimney Rock Village sent its sewage down the valley into Lake Lure's system. Hurricane Helene ended that in September 2024, when floodwaters destroyed the village's entire sewer.

Two years later, Chimney Rock treats its own sewage in a small temporary plant on Main Street. The plant discharges into the Broad River upstream of Lake Lure, in waters the state classifies as trout waters. Since July 2026, it has failed nearly every limit in its permit.

## A temporary plant

The 15,000-gallon-a-day package plant arrived in late May 2025 through the Army Corps of Engineers' Helene response. It began operating in July 2025 at 195 Main Street. Its [state permit](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=3764731&repo=WaterResources) expires Nov. 30, 2027.

The village has rebuilt part of its sewer: about 2,900 feet of new pipe and two lift stations serving about 30 customers, down from about 65 before the storm. A Terrace Drive extension permitted in June 2026 will add a fire station, an amphitheater and 24 homes. On Oct. 20, the village council advanced a roughly $1.32 million sewer project for Terrace and Crystal drives, with about 35 connections and construction that could begin after the first of the year, [Cops & Congress reported](https://anniedance.substack.com/p/watch-exclusive-chimney-rock-seeks). The village has said stormwater leaking into its new sewer is already a problem.

The plant had problems from the start. EPA's database records violations beginning in its first months and rates it in "Significant Noncompliance" for October through December 2025. In August 2025, state inspectors asked the village to repair a filter, finish a security fence and find sources of stormwater inflow. A November 2025 follow-up found no significant issues.

## Then it failed

In July 2026, the plant's results went bad and stayed bad. The state issued notices of violation for [July](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4694166&repo=WaterResources), [August](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4694167&repo=WaterResources) and [September](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4757190&repo=WaterResources).

By September, the plant's monthly average for BOD, a measure of untreated organic waste, was 205 milligrams per liter, against a limit of 30. Suspended solids averaged 280 against a limit of 30. Fecal coliform bacteria averaged 994 per 100 milliliters against a limit of 200. Weekly bacteria readings reached 4,500 to 6,000 against a limit of 400. EPA's compliance database has listed the plant in violation every quarter since late 2025.

The operator, RPB Systems of Asheville, has given the state the same explanation in three written responses. The sewage arriving at the plant is unusually strong, with BOD of 400 to 1,090 milligrams per liter, against a normal 100 to 200. Most of the flow comes from shops, restaurants and lodging rather than homes. The plant can't keep enough oxygen in its tanks, and summer heat stressed the bacteria that do the treating.

The operator has tried adjusting aeration, adding soda ash, feeding the bacteria, and pumping out and restarting the plant three times. It wrote that these brought "only minor improvements." By November, it still did not know where the strong waste was coming from. It suspected "something toxic" was entering the system, and it said it had asked the state for help.

DEQ staff "have also been in contact with the operator of their temporary wastewater treatment system to address issues," DEQ spokesperson Laura Oleniacz said in a Nov. 13 statement.

That pattern, very little flow but very strong waste, is common at small plants serving restaurants and food businesses. The usual remedy is finding the source and requiring pretreatment, such as grease traps, at the business.

Village officials have pointed in that direction. At a Sept. 22 council meeting, they said elevated grease levels in the sewer were affecting treatment, [WCAB reported](https://www.wcabnews.com/post/chimney-rock-advances-helene-recovery-projects-awaits-grants). They discussed strengthening the village's grease-control ordinance and enforcement and said they may require more advanced grease-control equipment at the two local restaurants.

## "Not a viable option"

In January 2026, before the failures began, the village filed a [Rehabilitation Strategy](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4287990&repo=WaterResources) with the state. It made its position on Lake Lure plain.

The village wrote that Lake Lure's system "is currently deemed a non-compliant system," under a consent order and moratorium "with no short-term end." To "safeguard the autonomy of the Village," it said, it "does not view reconnection" to Lake Lure "as a viable option." It added that before Helene, Lake Lure had refused repeated requests to give the village managerial control of its own sewer.

The village's preferred solution in that filing was its own permanent plant, sized at 120,000 gallons a day, with a target of late 2027. In December 2025, the state had [awarded the village](https://governor.nc.gov/news/press-releases/2025/12/11/governor-stein-deq-announce-270-million-resilient-water-infrastructure-projects-areas-damaged) $5 million for a new wastewater treatment plant and sewer resiliency improvements, plus $4.2 million to extend sewer lines to homes on septic systems.

## And yet, the negotiations

At the same time, the two towns are negotiating. Lake Lure Council minutes from Aug. 11, 2026 say the village has proposed a long-term agreement for 100,000 gallons a day and a bulk sewer rate. The talks are being handled with the UNC School of Government and DEQ, with a goal of an agreement around December. The village's rehabilitation strategy describes the talks as a way to secure "autonomy and managerial control" before any reconnection.

The choice is still open. At the village's Oct. 20 council meeting, its planned wastewater improvements included "either a connection to Lake Lure or a wastewater treatment plant," and officials said the wastewater decision would determine how much of its funding the village ultimately uses, Cops & Congress reported.

Lake Lure's [draft amended consent order](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4693963&repo=WaterResources) reserves 25,000 gallons a day for Chimney Rock. That is capacity Lake Lure cannot give anyone else, at a plant that ran over its permit limit this summer ([Part 1](/news/lake-lure-sewer-over-capacity)), for a partner that has told the state it doesn't plan to reconnect.

## One river, two plants

The result is two struggling treatment plants on the same river. Chimney Rock's discharges above the lake, and Lake Lure's below the dam. Each town is pursuing a permanent fix, and each is relying on state disaster loans and grants to pay for it.

Chimney Rock's flow is tiny, a few thousand gallons a day against Lake Lure's million. The Town's 2026 lake samples at Lake Lure Beach were within limits except for one elevated E. coli reading on June 3, before the village's plant began failing. But untreated-quality effluent is going into trout waters a short distance above a swimming lake, and no public record yet shows downstream sampling below the outfall.

## Where it goes from here

After four parts and hundreds of pages of state and federal records, the problem and the cure are both clear. What isn't clear is how fast the cure will come, or who will pay for it.

Every engineering report in the file agrees on what would fix Lake Lure's system. There are three steps, in this order:

1. **Keep the lake out of the sewer.** That means replacing the 1927 cast-iron line on the lake bottom with new sewer along the shore. Under the proposed order, the first phase is due in 2029 and the second in 2031, and the rest of the lake has no schedule yet.
2. **Build a plant that can remove ammonia.** That takes biological treatment, which the current plant has not had since 1991. The design is due to the state in March 2027, but the proposed order sets no construction date.
3. **Until then, don't add more than the plant can handle.** The plant already ran over its permitted flow this summer, while the Town was taking applications for the 84,000 gallons a day the state allowed after Helene.

The first two are years away. The third is a choice the Town and the state can make now.

Chimney Rock Village faces the same choice on a smaller scale. It can fix its temporary plant, build a permanent one of its own, or reconnect to Lake Lure. Whatever it decides will shape how much capacity Lake Lure needs, and how many treatment plants end up on one short stretch of river.

### Questions still open

- The state resumed citing Lake Lure's plant for violations in 2026, after pausing enforcement following Helene. Will the penalties it is holding ever be paid, or will they be forgiven when the order ends?
- Will the Town pause new sewer connections while the plant runs at or above its limit?
- Will Lake Lure and Chimney Rock end up with one regional solution, or two separate plants on the same river?
- Can about 660 sewer customers carry the debt for a fix estimated at $125 million, while the Town also faces a dam replacement estimated at $158 million or more?
