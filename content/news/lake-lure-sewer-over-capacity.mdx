*Based on the N.C. Department of Environmental Quality's permit files for the Lake Lure wastewater plant (NC0025381) and sewer system (WQCS00131), EPA's ECHO monitoring database, and Town Council minutes and packets. Every one of those records is searchable at [lakelureinsider.com/records](/records).*

LAKE LURE, N.C. — In June, July and August 2026, the Town of Lake Lure's wastewater plant took in more flow than its state permit allows. It was the first time since the current 0.995-million-gallon limit took effect in October 2022 that the plant had crossed it for even one month, according to federal monitoring data.

A little over a year earlier, state regulators had agreed to let the Town connect up to 84,000 more gallons a day of new sewage to the same plant.

The plant has been under a state sewer moratorium since March 2022. The moratorium exists because, in the words of the state statute behind it, the plant could not adequately treat the waste it was already receiving. State records reviewed by Lake Lure Insider show the extra capacity was approved after Hurricane Helene to help the town recover economically. Federal records show the plant kept breaking its pollution limits throughout that period, with no state violation notice in its file for any month of 2025.

## A moratorium for a reason

On March 15, 2022, the Asheville office of the N.C. Division of Water Resources put Lake Lure under a statutory moratorium. The letter cited "historical non-compliance" of both the Town's sewer lines and its treatment plant. No new sewer lines could connect until the Town was "consistently compliant" with both permits and received written notice from the state lifting the ban.

That has not happened. The moratorium is still in effect.

The root problem is the sewer itself. Most of the Town's roughly 14 miles of cast-iron sewer lies on the bottom of Lake Lure, laid when the lake was built in 1927. Lake water leaks in. In its [2022 permit review](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=2448451&repo=WaterResources), the state described the plant's discharge as "mostly lake water with an unknown amount of wastewater."

## "DEQ's offer"

On April 1, 2025, the Town's engineering firm, LaBella Associates, wrote to DEQ's Asheville office on the Town's behalf. The letter opened by thanking the department for its help after Helene and said the Town was "particularly grateful for DEQ's offer to ease conditions of the sewer moratorium." The purpose, the letter said, was to let the Town approve new developments expected to bring "economic growth."

The letter asked for 84,000 gallons a day. It also asked that up to 32,000 gallons a day from Chimney Rock Village, whose sewer Helene destroyed, be treated as returning flow rather than new flow.

The 84,000 figure was not measured. Because lake water makes the plant's real sewage flow impossible to isolate, the engineer worked backward from a population projection for 2050. That produced an estimated current "non-leakage" flow of about 384,000 gallons a day. The engineer then grew that number 4% a year for five years and asked for the difference.

On May 23, 2025, DEQ [approved the request](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=3868281&repo=WaterResources). "The Division approves your flow allocation request," Assistant Regional Supervisor Daniel Boss wrote. New flow would be granted through individual sewer-extension permits. Requests beyond 84,000 gallons would be reviewed case by case.

State law allows such exceptions. Under G.S. 143-215.67(b), the state may let a local government add waste during a moratorium if it has secured financing for improvements and the extra waste "will not result in any significant degradation" of the receiving water. The approval letter in DEQ's file does not describe any such finding.

## What the plant was doing at the time

The plant's own monthly reports, filed with the state and posted in EPA's [ECHO database](https://echodata.epa.gov/echo/eff_rest_services.download_effluent_chart?p_id=NC0025381&start_date=01%2F01%2F2022&end_date=10%2F31%2F2026&p_echo=Y), show what was happening while the allocation was being requested and used.

The plant's summer ammonia limit is 5.2 milligrams per liter. Ammonia is toxic to fish, and this plant has no biological treatment process that can remove it. In every month from April through October 2025, the plant's monthly average exceeded that limit, ranging from 9.8 to 18.3 milligrams per liter. July 2025, at 18.3, was 3.5 times the limit.

DEQ's file for the plant contains no notice of violation for any of those months. EPA's database lists the plant's compliance status as "Unknown" for every quarter from January 2025 through June 2026. The last state penalty order on record is dated Sept. 6, 2024, three weeks before Helene.

## Then the flow came

In spring 2026, flow to the plant rose sharply. April averaged 0.78 million gallons a day and May 0.98, compared with 0.41 and 0.46 a year earlier. The permit limit is 0.995.

June came in at 0.999. July hit 1.082 and August 1.047. DEQ issued violation notices for all three months, each also citing ammonia over the limit.

In an Aug. 13 reply, Town Manager Olivia Stewman told DEQ the Town was making manhole repairs "expected to reduce infiltration" and had awarded a bid for sewer replacement work. On Sept. 23, 900 gallons of sewage overflowed at the plant's influent lift station during heavy rain, according to a Sept. 29 DEQ notice.

The timing matches the lake's refill. Dam repairs kept Lake Lure about 15 feet low through the winter, and it was refilled in spring 2026. When the lake is higher, more water pushes into the old pipes. At the Nov. 19, 2025 work session, staff had warned Council that leakage could increase once the lake returned to full pond.

## Who wants the capacity

The Town Council adopted a Sewer Capacity Allocation Policy on Sept. 9, 2025. In-town connections are first come, first served and can be approved by staff. Out-of-town requests go to Council and pay $3.25 per gallon of daily capacity. An April 2026 amendment requires out-of-town projects to petition for annexation.

At a July 23, 2025 work session, Stewman told Council the Town had "received several applications for larger developments," according to the minutes. She described the approval as 87,000 gallons a day, granted in March; DEQ's letter says 84,000, granted May 23.

Town records show at least three large requests:

- **Simple Life**, a proposed community of 300-plus tiny homes near McDaniel and Herman Wilson roads in Bills Creek, asked for 34,000 gallons a day, about 40% of the allocation, with a $110,500 capacity fee. At a [Nov. 19, 2025 work session](https://www.townoflakelure.com/towncouncil/page/lake-lure-town-council-work-session-and-action-meeting-summary-wednesday-november), neighbors raised concerns about traffic, wells and emergency services, and a commissioner noted the project did not fit Town zoning. Council unanimously denied the request that day on the Town Manager's recommendation, as Annie Dance's Cops & Congress newsletter reported.
- **Lake Lure Senior Living**, a proposed 74-unit, 113-bedroom independent living facility at 890 Buffalo Creek Road, beside the Town's offices at The Landings, asked for 8,475 gallons a day, with a $27,534 fee. Its request was on Council's [March 10, 2026 agenda](https://www.townoflakelure.com/sites/default/files/fileattachments/town_council/page/9166/03-10-2026_town_council_regular_meeting_packet.pdf). The property's annexation went before Council in August and September 2026, and the developer filed a state sewer permit application in October.
- **Chimney Rock Village** has proposed a long-term agreement for 100,000 gallons a day, according to the minutes of Council's Aug. 11, 2026 meeting. That is more than the entire 84,000-gallon allocation and about a tenth of the plant's permitted capacity. The Town hopes to reach an agreement around December. A [draft amended state order](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4693963&repo=WaterResources) sets aside 25,000 gallons a day for the village. Yet in a January 2026 filing with DEQ, the village said it does not consider reconnecting to Lake Lure "a viable option" while the Town's system is non-compliant. It is seeking state money for its own 120,000-gallon plant instead.

## Why it matters

A treatment plant's pipes, pumps and tanks handle all the water that reaches them, sewage and lake water alike. When a plant already runs at or above its permitted flow, every added connection raises the odds of more overflows and more violations. In this case, those violations land in the Broad River just below the dam.

None of that means new connections must wait forever. The Town has a plan and some money to fix the sewer (Part 3 of this series covers both). But that plan's first major deadline, May 30, 2026, has already passed, and the state is preparing to extend it to 2031.
