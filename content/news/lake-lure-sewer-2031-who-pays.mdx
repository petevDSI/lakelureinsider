LAKE LURE, N.C. — Everyone involved agrees on the cure. Lake water is leaking into a sewer laid on the lake bottom in 1927, so the sewer has to be replaced, and then the treatment plant. The disagreement, as the record shows, is over how fast that can happen and who will pay for it.

## The plan, and the first deadline

In 2020, the Town and its engineers at LaBella Associates studied 11 ways to replace the lake-bottom sewer. They chose a "gravity-lift station" design that would rebuild the sewer along the shoreline, below the lake's normal level. Its estimated cost then was about $57 million.

The 2022 consent order with the state, a formal agreement that relaxes some pollution limits while repairs are made, set the first milestone. Phase I, replacing about 9,000 feet of sewer, was to be finished by May 30, 2026.

It was not. Helene is the reason the Town gives. But the Town's own correspondence shows the project had stalled well before the storm.

## Stalled before the storm

On June 20, 2024, Town Manager Olivia Stewman wrote to state officials with a status update.

The new shoreline sewer depended on a drain valve the state's dam safety program required in the base of the 100-year-old dam. The valve would let the lake be lowered about 20 feet instead of 12. It was supposed to be finished by October 2022. In June 2024, the letter said it was on track for the end of 2024, without a guarantee.

Without the valve, construction had largely stopped. The contractor did some preliminary work in Sunset Cove during the 2022–23 drawdown. Work planned for 2023–24 was abandoned. The Town said it would skip the 2024–25 season and begin "in earnest" in 2025–26.

Meanwhile, costs had climbed. The Town's funding stood at $27.5 million, and the gap between that and the projected cost had grown from $45 million to $75 million. The letter said the Town recognized that, without substantial new money, it "may never be financially feasible" to finish.

An attached fact sheet put the estimate at more than $100 million. It noted that the Town has fewer than 1,600 residents and about 660 sewer connections, and that it also faces a roughly $100 million dam replacement and a $35 million plant replacement. Both figures have since grown. In September 2026, WLOS reported a preliminary dam construction estimate of $158 million, and an independent estimate near $174 million.

Helene struck three months later.

## The new schedule

After the storm, the Town redesigned the first phase as a force-main system. It says that design covers with 7,800 feet of pipe what the old design needed 11,700 feet to do, at an estimated $12.2 million. The Town's September 2026 project page describes the first phase as about 11,600 feet of sewer with nine pump stations, and says major shoreline work is not expected until the 2027–28 drawdown. The state's funding list puts the full force-main project at $93.2 million and a new plant at $34.0 million. The Town says it has completed nearly $3 million in permanent sewer repairs since Helene.

In May 2026, the Town asked the state to amend the consent order. On Sept. 28, the state published [notice](https://www.deq.nc.gov/news/events/comment-peroid-opens-notification-intent-issue-consent-order-town-lake-lure-permit-nc0025381) of its intent to issue the amendment, proposed to take effect Dec. 1, 2026. The [draft](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4693963&repo=WaterResources) sets new milestones through 2031:

Two things are not on that schedule: construction of the new treatment plant, and the rest of the lake. Both are left to an undefined Phase III. In November 2024, the Town told state inspectors a new plant would be built within two years with federal disaster money.

Until then, the order's loosened limit on suspended solids, 200 milligrams per liter instead of 30, continues through 2031. Ammonia, the plant's most persistent violation, gets no new relief and no fix until a new plant is built.

## The money

In its May 2026 [application](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=4500718&repo=WaterResources), the Town estimated the full fix at about $125 million in 2024 dollars. It listed about $41.5 million it plans to use, including the original $12.5 million in state loans and grants, $8 million in federal ARPA funds, a $7 million loan from 2023, and 2026 Helene recovery money.

The Town also said it may decline about $26 million in offered loans, citing "potentially astronomical" sewer rates. Rates are already "among the highest in the State," the Town wrote, citing a UNC rates dashboard. It also said the state Local Government Commission might not approve more borrowing.

The Oct. 13, 2026 Council minutes show more offers. They include a state letter of intent for a $31 million loan with $6 million forgiven, which the Town is still "assessing," and several smaller Helene awards.

Some money comes with a clock. About $1.7 million in ARPA funds must be spent by Dec. 31, 2026, and about $13.7 million in 2026 state funds must be under contract by May 2, 2028.

## Penalties waiting to be forgiven

If the Town completes the amended order, the state will cancel the $38,882 in pre-2022 penalties it set aside. It will also cancel the $28,622 in new penalties that have built up under the order as of August 2026. If the order fails, all of it comes due at once.

DEQ says holding the penalties is deliberate. "The payment of civil penalties tied to past and ongoing violations is on hold until the completion of the order to allow the town to direct resources toward the necessary upgrades," DEQ spokesperson Laura Oleniacz said in a Nov. 13 statement. The agency's "ultimate goal in enforcement," she said, "is compliance with permit conditions."

To keep the order on track, the Town must send the state progress reports every June and December. Three are in the state's file: March 2023, November 2023 and December 2024. Each is a short email of a few sentences.

## What residents can do

The comment period on the amended order ran from Oct. 3 to Nov. 2, 2026. DEQ received six public comments, which are under review, and has not announced whether it will issue the order, Oleniacz said Nov. 13. The draft would have taken effect Dec. 1. The state's notice said a public meeting "may be held if there is a significant degree of public interest."

Two of the comments are in DEQ's public file. In a [joint letter](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?id=4766614&dbid=0&repo=WaterResources), Broad Riverkeeper David Caldwell and the conservation group MountainTrue said they did not oppose reasonable extensions for Helene but urged the state to require compliance sooner. They asked for firmer deadlines: new solids-handling equipment installed by the end of 2026, the first shoreline sewer work finished in stages starting in 2027, a construction date for the new plant, a schedule to reduce ammonia, and steady cuts under the relaxed solids and BOD limits. Because the state proposes to forgive the fines if the order is completed, they wrote, "the Town appears to have little incentive to avoid additional permit violations." They also asked for a public meeting.

The owner of River Creek Campground, a few miles downstream, [wrote in support](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?id=4766610&dbid=0&repo=WaterResources) of that letter and asked the state to "add timelines that hold the town more accountable to progress."

Residents can still ask Council how much of the new sewer capacity has been promised, and to whom, before more is allocated. They can also ask the Town to post its semi-annual progress reports, and ask DEQ to hold the public meeting the Riverkeeper requested.
