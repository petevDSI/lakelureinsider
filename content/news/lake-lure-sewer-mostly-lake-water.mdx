*This is Part 2 of a four-part series built from the N.C. Department of Environmental Quality's permit files, EPA monitoring data and Town records. [Part 1](/news/lake-lure-sewer-over-capacity) covered the plant running over its permitted flow after the state allowed new connections. Every record cited here is searchable at [lakelureinsider.com/records](/records).*

LAKE LURE, N.C. — Lake Lure's leaking lake-bottom sewer is no secret. Carolina Public Press wrote about it in 2022, and the Broad Riverkeeper tracked bacteria from the Helene spill 50 miles downriver in 2024.

But the state's records show three things that have not been reported:

- In progress reports to the state, the Town described its ammonia violations as "covered by our current SOC." The consent order does not relax the ammonia limit.
- DEQ's file contains no overflow report, volume estimate or violation notice for the raw sewage that reached the Broad River at two places after Helene.
- After Helene, the state stopped citing heavily damaged plants for violations. Lake Lure's ammonia violations went without a notice from the storm until June 2026, including the months when the state approved more flow.

How the system got here starts with a design decision made nearly a century ago.

When developers built Lake Lure in the 1920s, they laid the resort's sewer on the valley floor and flooded it. Nearly a century later, that pipe is still in service: about 14 miles of cast iron, running under as much as 100 feet of water to a treatment plant below the dam.

It leaks. Lake water seeps in through old joints and dozens of perimeter manholes, diluting the sewage on its way to the plant. In 2022, the state's permit writer summed up the result in a single line: until the sewer is repaired, the plant's discharge will remain "mostly lake water with an unknown amount of wastewater," according to the [2022 permit review](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=2448451&repo=WaterResources).

That one fact explains almost everything else in the record.

## A plant that can't do the job

The Town's plant opened in 1969 as a conventional biological plant, according to a 2021 history prepared for the state. Before then, the Town's sewage went straight into the Broad River.

Biological plants rely on bacteria to eat the waste. At Lake Lure, the incoming sewage was too cold and too diluted by lake water to keep them alive. In 1991, the plant was converted to physical-chemical treatment, which uses alum and polymer to settle out solids. It has no aeration and no biological treatment.

That conversion has a price. Removing ammonia, which is toxic to fish, requires the bacterial process the plant no longer has. Iron from the cast-iron pipe and the lake water also creates large amounts of sludge. A state inspection in November 2024 found the plant's digester working at about half its capacity because of structural problems.

## A decade of violations

The state's file holds at least 35 violation notices and penalty assessments from 2014 through 2017 alone, most for ammonia.

In 2017, the Town asked the state for a consent order, a formal agreement that relaxes some limits while a fix is built. Its stated fix was to pipe its sewage to Spindale's plant through a proposed "Green Line." In August 2018, the state returned the request. The Town had never pursued that plan, the letter said. The state ended its waiver from enforcement and noted the Town owed $29,789.68 in unpaid penalties.

The sewer itself kept failing too. On June 9, 2019, during a storm and power outage, about 119,000 gallons of raw sewage poured from the pump station at the dam into the Broad River. On Sept. 5, 2022, an equipment failure at a pump station on Buffalo Shoals Road sent 56,945 gallons into the river over nearly 10 hours.

When the plant's permit came up for renewal in 2022, the Southern Environmental Law Center objected. Its comments cited complaints to the Broad Riverkeeper about suds and a "septic tank type smell" downstream. The state replied that it could not investigate complaints that were never reported to it.

That same year, the mayor told Carolina Public Press the leakage had no negative effect on the lake. Four years later, a February 2026 EPA case study on the Town's state loan said the leakage "contributed to violations of the Town's wastewater permit effluent limits."

## The consent order

In April 2022, the Town and the state signed [Special Order by Consent S20-004](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=2301646&repo=WaterResources). It acknowledged that lake inflow was causing "inadequate treatment" and that the Town could not consistently meet its limits for ammonia and suspended solids.

The order loosened two limits while repairs were made. Suspended solids could rise from 30 to 200 milligrams per liter as a monthly average, and BOD, a measure of oxygen-consuming waste, from 30 to 60. Ammonia was not relaxed.

The Town owed $48,603 in old penalties. It paid $9,721 up front, and the remaining $38,882 was set aside, to be forgiven if the Town completed the order. The first phase of sewer replacement was due by May 30, 2026.

The ammonia violations continued. From July 2023 through September 2024, EPA's compliance database rated the plant in "Significant Noncompliance," its most serious category, every quarter.

The state cited some of them. It issued notices for ammonia over the limit in [April](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?id=3395686&dbid=0&repo=WaterResources) and [May](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?id=3395690&dbid=0&repo=WaterResources) 2024, according to DEQ.

The Town's own reports described it differently. In progress reports to the state in November 2023 and [December 2024](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=3583105&repo=WaterResources), the Town's public works director wrote that the plant was producing satisfactory samples "with the exception of NH3 which is covered by our current SOC." NH3 is ammonia, and the order's list of relaxed limits does not include it.

## Helene

On Sept. 27, 2024, Hurricane Helene sent floodwater over the Lake Lure dam. It destroyed the pump station that lifts sewage from the lake sewer to the plant 1,600 feet downstream.

Two days later, the Town's engineer, Brian Houston of LaBella Associates, described the scene to state officials [by email](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=3539104&repo=WaterResources). The pump building was gone. The equipment was unsalvageable. The sewer pipe that crosses the riverbed was broken and "discharging into the river." At the plant, all the electrical equipment had flooded and the tanks were underwater.

The email also asked a question on behalf of the Town's public services director: with the plant's basins "full of muddy river water and fish," could the Town wash them out with a fire hose and pump the contents into the Broad River? DEQ's file does not contain the answer.

Upstream, Helene also destroyed part of the lake sewer west of manhole 35B, including a section attached to the Flowering Bridge. In a later permit application, LaBella wrote that raw wastewater flowed untreated into the Broad River there as well, while repairs began.

The Town, LaBella and a contractor already working at the dam rebuilt the pump station on an emergency basis. When state inspectors [visited on Nov. 26, 2024](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?dbid=0&id=3590150&repo=WaterResources), the river crossing had been repaired and raw sewage was no longer entering the river. A temporary pump was feeding a partly working plant.

How long the sewage flowed, and how much, is not in the record. DEQ's file contains no five-day overflow report or violation notice for either Helene discharge. EPA's database rates the plant "No Violation Identified" for October through December 2024.

DEQ says the absence of violation notices was a decision. On Oct. 15, 2024, Gov. Roy Cooper issued [Executive Order 320](https://governor.nc.gov/executive-order-no-320/open), directing DEQ to help water and sewer systems recover. "Following that order," DEQ spokesperson Laura Oleniacz said in a Nov. 13 statement to Lake Lure Insider, the director of the Division of Water Resources "used enforcement discretion for facilities that were heavily damaged and out-of-compliance with permit conditions." DEQ continued to track the damaged facilities, she said.

The executive order itself does not mention enforcement or penalties. It directs DEQ to assess storm damage, make emergency loans and speed certain permits.

That period covers the months described in [Part 1](/news/lake-lure-sewer-over-capacity), when the plant's ammonia ran well over its limit through 2025 and into 2026 without a violation notice. It also covers May 2025, when the state approved the Town's request to add 84,000 gallons a day. According to DEQ staff, Oleniacz said, the extra allocation was requested "to allow the town to recover following Hurricane Helene, and to ensure infrastructure is adequate for projected growth."

"Enforcement has resumed," she said. The state has issued notices for [June](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?id=4621327&dbid=0&repo=WaterResources), [July](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?id=4693955&dbid=0&repo=WaterResources) and [August](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov/WaterResources/DocView.aspx?id=4705969&dbid=0&repo=WaterResources) 2026.

The river told a different story. In October 2024, the [Broad Riverkeeper](https://mountaintrue.org/watershed-report-brk/) reported very high bacteria levels just below Lake Lure. Over the following weeks, it tracked the contamination about 50 miles downstream, to the Broad River Greenway in Boiling Springs.

## A warning before the storm

Six months before Helene, a March 2024 state inspection of the sewer cited an unenforced grease-trap program, an overdue annual report and a broken high-level alarm at a pump station. The inspector also recorded a larger warning. The main line under the lake had been wrapped in 2011 and was past its 10-year life expectancy, and there was "a risk of a catastrophic failure" that would flood the plant.

That risk is still there.
